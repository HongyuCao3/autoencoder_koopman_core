\begin{abstract}
Multi-turn behavioral steering aims to hold a target property of a language model's replies
on target as the interaction unfolds.
However, the value of an intervention and the value of its timing are confounded in the
comparisons that motivate state-aware controllers.
Existing interventions, such as per-turn classifiers, fixed reminder schedules, one-shot
system prompts, and learned controllers, are scored against an unregulated baseline, so the
reported gain separates neither.
To fundamentally fill this gap, we model the multi-turn readout as a linear operator driven
by the intervention itself, and deliver what that operator buys together with where control
built on it stops paying.
Our study contributes three instruments: 1) operator identification under randomized
intervention, aiming to decouple the action from the state; 2) equal-cost closed-loop
evaluation, intending to place a model-predictive controller against the strongest open-loop
schedule and against hand-written feedback rules; 3) a planning-headroom criterion,
dedicating to settle whether re-timing the same actions buys anything before closed-loop
compute is spent.
Across four multi-turn settings, delay-embedded memory is required in every informative
comparison, while nonlinear state encoders move the fit by less than the paired uncertainty
and flip sign between tasks.
No closed-loop arm beats its equal-cost best fixed schedule in any setting, and where the
bound is computable, a controller granted full hindsight over the same readout ties as well.
The criterion reproduces that verdict at no generation cost, and it is one-directional: it
licenses a decision not to invest, and its non-zero side stays untested.
\end{abstract}
