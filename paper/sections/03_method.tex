\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

KOMPAS consists of an offline dynamics model and an online
receding-horizon controller. Each completed response is mapped to the
scalar attribute being controlled, recent attribute measurements form a
finite-memory state, and a command-conditioned Koopman model predicts how
that state evolves under a prompt instruction. At inference time, the
controller rolls candidate commands forward through the learned dynamics,
scores their predicted trajectories against the desired attribute, executes
the best command, and replans after observing the next response. The frozen
LLM $p_\theta$ is never updated, and candidate evaluation requires no
additional query to it. Figure~\ref{fig:method} summarizes this pipeline.

\subsection{Observable-State Koopman Dynamics}
\label{sec:model}

The controller does not model text directly. Each completed response $o_t$
is mapped by the task-specific measurement function $V$ from
Section~\ref{sec:problem_modeling} to a scalar attribute $y_t=V(o_t)$.
The particular definition of $V$ depends on the task and is given in the
experimental setup; the dynamics model uses only the resulting scalar
measurements.

A single measurement generally does not retain enough temporal context to
predict the next one. The same value $y_t$, for example, may occur along an
increasing or decreasing trajectory and therefore lead to different future
responses. We represent this short-term history with delay coordinates,
\begin{equation}
    s_t=
    \begin{bmatrix}
        y_t & y_{t-1} & \cdots & y_{t-L}
    \end{bmatrix}^{\top}
    \in\mathbb{R}^{L+1},
    \qquad
    y_t=C s_t,
    \label{eq:memory_state}
\end{equation}
where $L\geq 1$ is the number of previous measurements retained and
$C=[1,0,\ldots,0]$ reads the current attribute from the state. We use
$s_t$ as a finite-memory approximation to the information in the dialogue
history that is relevant for predicting the attribute; it is not intended
to reconstruct the full textual state.

The controller acts through a scalar command rather than directly over the
space of text strings. A fixed prompting protocol maps a command
$c_t\in\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathbb{R}$ to the textual instruction
$u_t=\mathcal{P}(x,\mathcal{H}_t,c_t)$. Thus, after fixing
$\mathcal{P}$, the controlled interaction can be viewed at the attribute
level as a transition from $(s_t,c_t)$ to $s_{t+1}$.

This controlled transition induces a command-conditioned Koopman operator
on observables. For an observable $f$, we define
$(\mathcal{K}_c f)(s)
=
\mathbb{E}[f(s_{t+1})\mid s_t=s,c_t=c]$.
The operator is linear in $f$ even when the underlying prompt--response
interaction is nonlinear. We use the delay coordinates in $s_t$ as a finite
dictionary of observables and approximate the induced dynamics with the
affine model
\begin{equation}
    \widehat{s}_{t+1}
    =
    K s_t+B c_t+b,
    \qquad
    \widehat{y}_{t+1}
    =
    C\widehat{s}_{t+1}.
    \label{eq:koopman_model}
\end{equation}
Here $K\in\mathbb{R}^{(L+1)\times(L+1)}$ propagates the memory state,
$B\in\mathbb{R}^{(L+1)\times 1}$ captures the effect of the command, and
$b\in\mathbb{R}^{L+1}$ is a bias term. This parameterization deliberately
shares one transition matrix across commands and lets the command enter
through a single linear channel. These are modeling choices rather than
consequences of Koopman linearity, and we do not assume that the frozen LLM
admits an exact finite-dimensional Koopman representation.

More generally, Koopman dynamics may be defined on lifted coordinates
$E_\phi(s_t)$ and decoded back to the measurement space. Our main model uses
the identity lifting, $E_\phi(s_t)=s_t$, so the learned dynamics operate
directly on the delay coordinates. We retain a learned lifting as an
experimental comparison and give its training objective in
Appendix~\ref{app:A1_estimator}. In this sense, the reported model is also a
linear controlled regression on delay coordinates; the Koopman formulation
places the identity and learned dictionaries within the same observable-space
framework and enables closed-form multi-step prediction.


\subsection{Offline System Identification}
\label{sec:learning}

We fit the dynamics model from recorded interaction trajectories. Sliding a
window of $L+1$ measurements over a trajectory produces transition tuples
$(s_t,c_t,s_{t+1})$, where $c_t$ is the command applied before observing the
successor state.

Because $K$, $B$, and $b$ enter the one-step prediction linearly, the
identity-lifting model (i.e., the state is used directly without an additional
feature transformation) is estimated by least squares,
\begin{equation}
    (\widehat K,\widehat B,\widehat b)
    =
    \arg\min_{K,B,b}
    \sum_t
    \left\|
        s_{t+1}-(Ks_t+Bc_t+b)
    \right\|_2^2.
    \label{eq:koopman_fit}
\end{equation}
The fit is performed entirely offline and does not modify the parameters
$\theta$ of the target LLM. The range in which the learned dynamics can be
trusted is consequently determined by the state--command combinations
represented in the recorded trajectories. In particular, prediction under a
new command history relies on generalization beyond the combinations observed
during training.

For comparison, we also consider a learned-lifting variant (i.e., the state is
first mapped by a trainable encoder into a feature space before applying the
same affine dynamics). This variant jointly learns the representation and the
state transition, with its training objective given in
Appendix~\ref{app:A1_estimator}. The main method uses the identity-lifting
model and the closed-form least-squares estimator above.


\subsection{Receding-Horizon Prompt Control}
\label{sec:control_method}

At inference time, candidate commands are evaluated in the learned dynamics
rather than by querying the LLM. Let $\mathcal{C}_t\subseteq\mathcal{C}$ be
the finite nonempty set of candidate commands available at step $t$.
For each candidate $c\in\mathcal{C}_t$, we hold that command fixed only
inside a hypothetical $H$-step rollout initialized from the observed state
$s_t$. The predicted state obeys
$\widehat{s}_{t|t}(c)=s_t$ and
$\widehat{s}_{t+h|t}(c)
=
K\widehat{s}_{t+h-1|t}(c)+Bc+b$,
with
$\widehat y_{t+h|t}(c)=C\widehat{s}_{t+h|t}(c)$.

Because the transition is affine, the rollout has the closed form
\begin{equation}
    \widehat{s}_{t+h\mid t}(c)
    =
    K^h s_t
    +
    \sum_{j=0}^{h-1}
    K^j(Bc+b).
    \label{eq:closed_form_rollout}
\end{equation}
Candidate evaluation therefore requires only linear propagation through the
identified model and adds no call to $p_\theta$.

Each candidate is scored by the deviation of its predicted trajectory from
the desired attribute,
\begin{equation}
    \widehat J_t(c)
    =
    \sum_{h=1}^{H}
    w_h
    \left|
        \widehat y_{t+h\mid t}(c)-r
    \right|^2,
    \qquad
    c_t^\star
    \in
    \arg\min_{c\in\mathcal{C}_t}
    \widehat J_t(c),
    \quad
    w_h\geq 0.
    \label{eq:predictive_control}
\end{equation}
Scoring the whole predicted trajectory allows the selected command to differ
from the reference while the attribute is still moving toward it.

The controller then executes only the selected command $c_t^\star$.
Specifically, it constructs
$u_t=\mathcal{P}(x,\mathcal{H}_t,c_t^\star)$ and sends the instruction to
the frozen LLM. After receiving the actual response $o_{t+1}$, the controller
measures $y_{t+1}=V(o_{t+1})$, shifts this observation into the memory state
to obtain $s_{t+1}$, and repeats the candidate evaluation from the updated
state. This observe--predict--select--execute cycle continues until the
interaction budget is exhausted.

Importantly, the assumption that a candidate command $c$ is held fixed applies
only to its hypothetical rollout used for comparison. The executed command
sequence is receding-horizon: after every real interaction, the controller
re-observes the system and may select a different command at the next step.


\subsection{Why Prediction Accuracy Matters}
\label{sec:prediction_control_bound}

Predictive control is useful only when ranking candidates under the learned
model approximately preserves their ranking under the true interaction.
The following result makes this connection explicit for the held-command
comparison performed at each control step.

For a candidate $c$, let $y_h^c$ denote the realized attribute after
$h$ further interactions under the same held command, and let
$J_t(c)=\sum_{h=1}^{H}w_h|y_h^c-r|^2$ be its realized trajectory cost.
Suppose the one-step state residual along candidate rollouts is bounded by
$\varepsilon_{\mathrm{dyn}}$, the readout residual is bounded by
$\varepsilon_{\mathrm{rec}}$, and the readout is Lipschitz on the region
visited by the rollouts. Then the resulting $h$-step attribute prediction
error is bounded by some $\Delta_h$, and the command selected by
Eq.~\eqref{eq:predictive_control} satisfies
\begin{equation}
    J_t(c_t^\star)
    \leq
    \min_{c\in\mathcal{C}_t} J_t(c)
    +
    2\delta_J,
    \label{eq:selection_bound}
\end{equation}
where $\delta_J$ is the corresponding bound on the difference between
predicted and realized candidate costs. Appendix~\ref{app:A6_selection_bound}
gives the explicit forms of $\Delta_h$ and $\delta_J$ and the proof.

The result is deliberately local: it concerns candidate selection at one
replanning step and does not assert convergence of the full closed loop.
Its role is to connect the modeling and control objectives: more accurate
multi-step rollouts make the command selected from the candidate set closer
to the best command that could have been chosen under the realized
trajectory costs.
