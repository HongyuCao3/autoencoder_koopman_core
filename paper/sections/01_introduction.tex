\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Multi-turn interaction turns a reply attribute of a frozen language model into a sequence with inertia.
The attribute may be a word count, a sentence count, a readability level, a sentiment polarity, or whether a formatting constraint is met.
What one reply does on such an attribute carries into the next reply, instead of being drawn fresh at every turn.
Instruction following in particular is measured to decay over a dialogue, so a constraint met early is not met for free later.
% CITE: li2024instability
Deployments need to hold such an attribute at a target, such as a length budget or a reading level, across every turn.
Holding a target requires steering, and inertia complicates steering.
The same current reading can sit on a rising path or on a falling path, and the next reply differs between the two.
A controller that reads one reply at a time cannot tell those two futures apart.

Prior literature partially addresses this control problem along three lines.\\
\textbf{1) Latent-space controllers.}
Activation steering and its feedback variants, control barrier functions, and latent-space predictive control all act inside the network.
They adjust activations, logits, or hidden states at every generated token.
% CITE: nguyen2026pidsteering, miyaoka2024cbfllm, tran2026barriersteer, cheng2024liseco, skifstad2026alqr, kong2024recontrol, wang2026tmpc, hu2025nbfllm
However, \uline{each of them needs white-box access to the target model and takes one token as its clock.}
The lever a deployment holds is one instruction per turn against a black-box API.\\
\textbf{2) Prompt-level remedies.}
Work on multi-turn instability counters it at the prompt: a system prompt written once, a reminder on a fixed schedule, or a template fired by a per-turn detector.
% CITE: li2024instability, dongre2025driftnomore
However, \uline{these remedies are stateless: none of them predicts what the next instruction will do to the next reply.}
The instruction is chosen from the current reply alone, and the two futures above receive the same instruction.\\
\textbf{3) Control-theoretic views of language models.}
A third line writes prompting as a control problem and studies reachability, controllability, and steerability.
Related work fits linear dynamics to token or embedding trajectories for analysis and for safety classification.
% CITE: bhargava2023magicword, cheng2026genctrl, fan2026steerable
% CITE-TODO(bib): akrout2026distinguishability (arXiv 2607.28667), dmd2026safety (arXiv 2608.19579) for the token/embedding-trajectory sentence
However, \uline{these results are reachability statements, theoretical bounds, or detectors at the token level; none identifies a model that maps one instruction to the next reply's attribute.}
What is missing is a dynamics model at the interaction level that uses only what a black-box caller can observe and carries the instruction as an explicit input.

The quantity a controller cares about is a property of a whole reply, not of any token inside it.
A verifier scores the reply once it is complete, never partway through generation.
The natural clock of the problem therefore ticks once per exchange.
Some verifiers are programs that count words or check a format; others are rater models that score a level or a polarity.
A black-box caller observes nothing else: the verifier's score is the only signal returned, so the state of any model must be built from these scores.
Controlling a room's temperature needs no model of each air molecule.
Controlling a reply's attribute needs no model of each token, and no access to the hidden state that produced it.

\textbf{Our Perspective: An Interaction-Level Koopman Perspective.}
We treat one prompt--response exchange as one step of a controlled dynamical system.
The intuition is that the next reading decomposes into three parts: what recent history carries forward, what the current instruction pushes, and a constant offset.
The state is the window of the last few readings, so a reading on a rising path and the same reading on a falling path become two different states.
A fixed prompting protocol turns a scalar command into the instruction text, which gives the instruction a coordinate the model can read.
Linear predictors with inputs, fit on observables and paired with model predictive control, are an established route for nonlinear systems that must be planned over in real time.
% CITE: korda2018koopman  % TODO(bib): Korda & Mezic, Automatica 2018, not yet in main.bib
The same route fits this setting, since a window of verifier readings is a dictionary of observables and the command is the input.
On this window we fit an affine one-step model by least squares on recorded exchanges.
In its linear form this model is a delay regression with an exogenous input, and we do not claim otherwise.
The operator view adds three things. The window is one choice of dictionary and a learned lifting is another; a horizon of steps has a closed form; predictive control applies unchanged.
A lifting maps the window through a learned feature map and applies the same affine step there.
We report the identity lifting throughout and keep a learned lifting as a comparison.
The controller rolls every candidate command forward through this model, scores the predicted trajectory against the target, executes the best command, and replans after the next reply.
Scoring a candidate costs a matrix product and a readout, with no extra query to the language model.
Only the first command is executed, and the next selection starts from the observed reply rather than from a predicted one.
\textbf{The key insight is that scoring on the predicted trajectory lets the controller send a command that differs from the target while the attribute is still moving.}
A model that predicts well also selects well: the cost of the chosen command exceeds the best available cost by at most a term set by the prediction error.
This yields \textbf{KO}opman dyna\textbf{M}ics modeling and \textbf{P}redictive control of \textbf{A}ttributes at the prompt \textbf{S}tep, or \textbf{KOMPAS}.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item We model the multi-turn dynamics of a reply attribute from verifier readings alone, with no access to the model's hidden state.
