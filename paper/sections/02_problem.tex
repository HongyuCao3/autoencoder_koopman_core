\section{Problem Formulation}
\label{sec:problem}

Two objectives share one interaction model: predicting the trajectory of a measured attribute over several exchanges, and driving that attribute to a target through prompt-level commands.

\subsection{Modeling}
\label{sec:problem_modeling}

At interaction step \(t\), a frozen large language model receives a task input, the accumulated dialogue history, and a textual control instruction, and produces the next response. The resulting input--output interaction is
\begin{equation}
o_{t+1}\sim
p_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x,\mathcal{H}_t,u_t),
\qquad
y_{t+1}=V(o_{t+1}),
\label{eq:interaction}
\end{equation}
Here \(x\) denotes the task input, \(p_{\theta}\) the frozen LLM, \(\mathcal{H}_t\) the dialogue history available at step \(t\), and \(u_t\) the textual instruction supplied by the controller. The response \(o_{t+1}\) is text, while \(V\) is a task-specific verifier that maps the completed response to a scalar attribute \(y_{t+1}\), such as sentence length or sentiment polarity. We track one such attribute at a time, and a step corresponds to one complete prompt--response exchange. The verifier output is the only signal the controller observes, so the measured attribute \(y_t\) serves both for identification and for stating the objective.

Control decisions depend on the consequences of an instruction over several future turns. The model is therefore evaluated by its multi-step prediction error \(\ell_h=\bigl(y_{t+h}-\widehat y_{t+h\mid t}\bigr)^2\) for \(h=1,\ldots,H\). Here \(\widehat y_{t+h\mid t}\) is the prediction of the attribute at step \(t+h\) from information available at step \(t\), and \(y_{t+h}\) is the realized measurement. The error should remain small throughout the horizon \(H\), rather than only at the one-step prediction \(h=1\).

\subsection{Control}
\label{sec:problem_control}

At each interaction step, the controller selects a scalar command \(c_t\), which determines the textual instruction \(u_t\) applied to the LLM. Over a finite horizon of \(H\) steps, the control objective is
\begin{equation}
J_t
=
\sum_{h=1}^{H}
\bigl(r-y_{t+h}\bigr)^2.
\label{eq:control_objective}
\end{equation}
Here \(r\in\mathbb{R}\) denotes the desired value of the tracked attribute, and \(e_t=r-y_t\) is the corresponding tracking error. Predictive control replaces the future measurements \(y_{t+h}\), which are unavailable at decision time, by their predictions \(\widehat y_{t+h\mid t}\) when selecting the commands.

The controlled quantity is continuous, whereas the experiments report constraint satisfaction. The two views meet in the task-specific feasible set \(\mathcal{Y}\): a response satisfies the constraint when \(y_t\in\mathcal{Y}\), and the reference is chosen such that \(r\in\mathcal{Y}\). Driving \(y_t\) toward \(r\) is therefore the mechanism through which the controller raises the rate of feasible responses.

The reference \(r\) specifies the desired output, whereas the command \(c_t\) is the action the controller encodes as the instruction \(u_t\). A direct target-following controller sets \(c_t=r\); predictive control allows \(c_t\neq r\), which requires a state that retains enough interaction history for multi-step prediction.
