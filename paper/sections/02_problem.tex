\section{Problem Formulation}
\label{sec:problem}

\subsection{Modeling}
\label{sec:problem_modeling}

Let $x$ denote a task input and $p_{\theta}$ a frozen large language
model (LLM). We track one output attribute at a time, such as sentence
length or sentiment polarity. At interaction step $t$, the model has
produced a dialogue history $\mathcal{H}_t$, and a controller supplies
a textual instruction $u_t$. A step is one complete prompt-response
exchange.

The next response and its measured attribute are
\begin{equation}
    o_{t+1}\sim
    p_{\theta}(\,\cdot\mid x,\mathcal{H}_t,u_t),
    \qquad
    y_{t+1}=V(o_{t+1}),
    \label{eq:interaction}
\end{equation}
Here $V$ is a task-specific verifier that reads a finished reply into
a scalar attribute. The reply $o_{t+1}$ is text; $y_{t+1}$ is its
measured attribute value. A caller outside the model sees $y_{t+1}$ and
nothing else of the model's internals. The activations that produced the
reply are out of reach, so the reading of the verifier is both the signal
available and the quantity the objective is stated in.

A model of this interaction is useful only if it stays accurate over
several future steps. A controller reasons about an instruction's
consequences several turns ahead, not only the very next one. We
therefore state the modeling objective as multi-step prediction
accuracy: the model's forecast should track the actual attribute
across a horizon of $H$ steps,
\begin{equation}
    \ell_h
    =
    \big(y_{t+h}-\widehat y_{t+h\mid t}\big)^2,
    \qquad h=1,\ldots,H,
    \label{eq:modeling_objective}
\end{equation}
Here $\widehat y_{t+h\mid t}$ is the model's forecast of the attribute
at step $t+h$, made from information available at step $t$, and
$y_{t+h}$ is the actual reading. The discrepancy $\ell_h$ should stay
small for every $h$ up to $H$, not only at $h=1$.
Section~\ref{sec:model} specifies how this forecast is
formed and what is held fixed to make it well defined.

\subsection{Control}
\label{sec:problem_control}

Let $r\in\mathbb{R}$ denote the desired value of the tracked attribute.
The tracking error is $e_t=r-y_t$, and the control objective is to
drive this error toward zero within a finite interaction budget. At
each step the controller's action is a scalar command $c_t$, which
determines the instruction $u_t$ applied to the model.

The tracked quantity is a number, and what the control experiments report
is a rate of constraint satisfaction. The two meet through the task's own
feasible set $\mathcal{Y}$: a reply counts as satisfying the constraint when
its reading $y_t$ lands in $\mathcal{Y}$, and $r$ is chosen inside
$\mathcal{Y}$. Driving the tracking error toward zero is therefore the means,
and the reported satisfaction rate is the outcome.

The desired output $r$ and the applied command $c_t$ play distinct
roles: $r$ states the objective, and $c_t$ is the controller's choice.
In target-following control, $c_t$ equals $r$. Predictive control
instead lets $c_t$ differ from $r$ to compensate for a predicted
response, which requires a state that carries enough history to
support that prediction.
